# Instructional structure

The viewer should always be able to answer four questions: What am I learning right now?
Where does it sit in the outline? What did I just learn? Why does the next part follow?
Every rule here serves one of those four.

## The sequence

| Stage | Narration | Visual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | Learning goal, why it matters to this audience, and the outline: every topic title, in order. A short hook is allowed if it does not push the outline out. | The home visual or outline strip with every topic named. |
| Announce | Name the topic with its outline title, word for word. | The outline item lights up, or the chapter heading changes. |
| Introduce | One or two sentences: the question this topic answers and what it builds on. | Orient the viewer to the figure, example, or context they are about to use. |
| Explain | Ideas in prerequisite order. Define a term before reasoning with it. One example where it helps. | Reveal each piece as the narration reaches it. Keep the context needed to read it. |
| Wrap up | One or two sentences stating the takeaway. | Hold or simplify the figure into its conclusion. This is where the topic's showpiece lands. |
| Bridge | Say how this topic connects to the next one, then name the move. | A visible topic change: the outline highlight moves, the heading changes. |
| Conclusion | Return to the outline and connect the takeaways into one model. No new claims. | The completed outline or the integrated figure. |

The last topic's bridge leads into the conclusion, not into an invented extra topic.

### Short videos

In a video under about ninety seconds, one sentence may do two jobs: an announcement can
also introduce (`[announce+introduce]`), and a wrap-up can also bridge. Keep every function
audible. Do not drop the roadmap or the wrap-ups to save time. If the content does not fit,
cut a topic (see Scope below).

### Avoiding ceremony

The structure should be audible without sounding like a form being filled in. Vary the
wording of announcements and bridges, but never the topic titles themselves. "Next: the
pooled trend" and "That brings us to the pooled trend" both work. "Moving on to our next
exciting section" does not, because it names nothing.

## Topics and scenes are different things

A **topic** is an item in the outline. A **scene** is a visual unit. They are tracked with
separate IDs.

- One topic can span several scenes. A scene change inside a topic is not a topic change, and
  the visual should keep the outline marker steady through it.
- A topic can change while the same figure stays on screen. The topic change still needs its
  spoken announcement and a visible marker (the outline highlight moves), even though the
  figure does not.
- A scene holds narration from only one topic. When a figure carries over a topic boundary,
  end the scene at the boundary and start a new one with the same figure and `Stays:` listing
  it.
- Topic titles stay fixed for orientation. A scene may also carry a takeaway heading ("Within
  each species, the trend reverses"), which is how a claim title from academic slides fits
  beside a stable outline.

## Bridges

A bridge states the actual relationship between two topics. Pick the one the material supports:

| Relationship | Shape of the bridge |
| --- | --- |
| Prerequisite | "Now that we can read a contour plot, we can read this one." |
| Cause and effect | "That pooled trend has a cause, and the cause is in the colors." |
| Comparison | "Bill shape separates two species. Body size separates the third." |
| Application | "Here is that idea applied to the field data." |
| Chronology | "That was the first season. The next one changed the picture." |
| Scale change | "That is one bird. Here are three hundred and forty two." |
| Synthesis | "Each of these pieces answers part of the opening question." |

Do not manufacture contrast or causation to make a bridge sound punchy. If two topics are
simply the next items in a list, say so plainly.

## Ordering

1. List what the viewer must already understand for each objective.
2. Order topics so every prerequisite comes first. A figure type the audience cannot read yet
   is a prerequisite too: a novice audience gets a short topic on reading a contour plot before
   the contour plot carries an argument. An expert audience skips it.
3. Lock terminology in the teaching plan. One name per concept for the whole video; define each
   acronym the first time it is spoken.

The video follows teaching order, which may differ from the order of the source document. A
research-results talk still uses this structure. Its topics follow the research question:
question, approach, finding, interpretation, limits. Results, interpretation, and limitations
stay in separate, clearly labeled places.

## Scope

Accuracy and comprehension come before covering everything. When the content does not fit the
duration:

1. Say so in the teaching plan before drafting, with the estimate.
2. Offer concrete options: cut a named topic, split into two videos, or extend the runtime.
3. Do not compress prerequisite explanations or speed the narration to make it fit.

A requested point that belongs to no topic does not become an aside. Make it a topic with its
own five roles if there is time, or move it to the plan's scope exclusions and tell the user why.
